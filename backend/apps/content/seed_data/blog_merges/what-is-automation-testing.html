How to Measure Automation ROI (The Math That Gets Budget Approved)

If you're a tester proposing automation to a manager, you need numbers, not enthusiasm. Here is the ROI model that actually gets approved.

The Simple ROI Formula

text
ROI = (Time saved per cycle × Cycles per year × Hourly cost) − Setup cost

Worked example:

  • Manual regression: 40 hours per sprint × 24 sprints per year = 960 hours/year

  • Automated regression: 4 hours of maintenance per sprint × 24 = 96 hours/year

  • Time recovered: 864 hours/year = 108 person-days

  • Loaded cost per QA hour: ₹1,500

  • Annual savings: ₹12,96,000

  • Setup cost (framework + first suite): 320 hours = ₹4,80,000

  • First-year net benefit: ₹8,16,000. Payback: ~4.5 months.

That is a ROI every manager will approve. The trick is that the second and third years have no setup cost, so ROI compounds.

What to Include (and What Managers Care About)

 
 
Metric What It Shows
Hours saved per release Direct cost savings
Defects caught before production Cost of fix: in-house vs production. A production bug costs 5–30× more than the same bug caught in QA
Release frequency increase More releases = more business value
MTTD reduction Faster feedback = faster fixes
Regression coverage increase % of critical paths automated
Onboarding time for new QA Automated suites document behaviour better than wikis

The ROI Traps That Kill Budget Approvals

  1. Counting flaky tests as passing — the ROI disappears the moment the manager sees a production bug that automation "covered."

  2. Ignoring maintenance cost — automation is not free after setup; plan for 20–30% of initial effort per year in maintenance.

  3. Automating the wrong things — automating exploratory or one-off tests is negative ROI.

  4. No reporting — if the dashboard doesn't exist, the savings don't exist for the manager.
