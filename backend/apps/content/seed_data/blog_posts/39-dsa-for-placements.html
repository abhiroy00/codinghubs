Most coding rounds in campus and off-campus placements test data structures and algorithms (DSA). The topic list can feel endless, and many students waste weeks jumping between random problems. This roadmap for DSA for placements tells you what to study, in what order, and how to practise so that your preparation actually shows up in interviews.

Step 0: Pick one language and stick to it

Use C++, Java or Python, whichever you know best. Learn its standard library well: dynamic arrays, hash maps, sets, sorting, stacks, queues and priority queues. Switching languages mid-preparation costs time.

Step 1: Time and space complexity

Before solving problems, understand Big O notation: O(1), O(log n), O(n), O(n log n), O(n²). Learn to estimate the complexity of loops and recursion. Interviewers almost always ask, "What is the time complexity of your solution? Can you do better?"

Step 2: Arrays and strings

These appear in a large share of placement questions. Learn:

  • Traversal, prefix sums and frequency counting.
  • Two pointers: pair sums in sorted arrays, removing duplicates, reversing.
  • Sliding window: longest substring without repeating characters, maximum sum subarray of size k.
  • Kadane's algorithm for maximum subarray sum.
  • String manipulation: palindromes, anagrams, reversing words.

Step 3: Hashing

Hash maps and sets turn many O(n²) solutions into O(n). Practise two-sum, counting frequencies, finding duplicates, grouping anagrams and the longest consecutive sequence.

Step 4: Sorting and searching

  • Understand how merge sort and quick sort work and their complexities.
  • Binary search on sorted arrays, and "binary search on the answer" problems (such as minimum capacity or square root).

Step 5: Recursion and backtracking

Recursion is the foundation for trees, graphs and dynamic programming. Practise factorial, Fibonacci, power, generating subsets and permutations, and classic backtracking such as N-Queens and combination sum. Learn to draw recursion trees.

Step 6: Linked lists

Reverse a list, detect a cycle (fast and slow pointers), find the middle, merge two sorted lists, and remove the nth node from the end. These test pointer handling and edge cases.

Step 7: Stacks and queues

Balanced parentheses, next greater element (monotonic stack), implementing a queue with stacks, and sliding window maximum (deque). Stacks appear often in problems involving "nearest" or "next" elements.

Step 8: Trees

  • Binary tree traversals: inorder, preorder, postorder and level order.
  • Height, diameter, balanced tree check, lowest common ancestor.
  • Binary search trees: search, insert, validate, kth smallest.

Most tree problems are recursion in disguise.

Step 9: Heaps

Use priority queues for "top k" problems, merging k sorted lists and finding the median of a stream.

Step 10: Graphs

  • Representations: adjacency list and matrix.
  • BFS and DFS, connected components, cycle detection.
  • Topological sort for dependency problems.
  • Shortest paths: BFS for unweighted graphs, Dijkstra's algorithm for weighted graphs.
  • Grid problems such as number of islands and rotting oranges.

Step 11: Dynamic programming

Start only after recursion is comfortable. Learn to identify overlapping subproblems, then move from recursion to memoisation to tabulation. Core problems: climbing stairs, house robber, coin change, longest common subsequence, 0/1 knapsack and longest increasing subsequence.

Step 12: Greedy and bit manipulation (as time allows)

Activity selection, interval scheduling and jump game for greedy; checking powers of two, counting set bits and the single-number XOR trick for bits.

How many problems should you solve?

Quality matters more than quantity. Around 150–250 well-chosen problems, covering every pattern above, is enough for most service and many product company placements. Aim for a mix of roughly 40% easy, 50% medium and 10% hard. Top product companies may need more depth on medium and hard problems.

How to practise effectively

  1. Try each problem for 20–30 minutes before looking at hints.
  2. If stuck, read only the approach, then code it yourself.
  3. After solving, write down the pattern and the key idea in a notebook.
  4. Re-solve problems you struggled with after a week.
  5. Practise explaining your approach aloud, as you would in an interview.
  6. Take timed mock tests to get used to pressure.

In the interview

  • Clarify the problem and ask about edge cases.
  • Explain a brute-force approach first, then optimise.
  • Write clean code with meaningful variable names.
  • Test with examples, including edge cases like empty input.
  • State time and space complexity.

Do not forget the rest

Placements also test aptitude, core subjects (OOP, DBMS, operating systems, computer networks), SQL, projects and communication. Balance DSA with these. See how to crack your first software developer interview and practise on Coding Hubs interview preparation.

===FAQ=== Q: Which language is best for DSA? A: C++, Java and Python are all accepted in most coding rounds. Choose the one you know best and learn its standard library thoroughly. Q: How long does it take to prepare DSA for placements? A: With one to two hours of daily practice, most students can cover the core topics in about three months. Starting earlier gives time for revision and harder problems. Q: Is DSA required for web development jobs? A: Many companies include at least a basic DSA round even for web development roles, especially for freshers. Arrays, strings and hashing are the most important. Q: How many DSA questions are enough for placements? A: Around 150 to 250 well-chosen problems covering all major patterns is enough for many companies, if you understand each solution deeply.
